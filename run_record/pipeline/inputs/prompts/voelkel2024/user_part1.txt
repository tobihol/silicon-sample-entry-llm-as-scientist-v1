DEMOCRACY / PARTISAN-ATTITUDES megastudy, not a climate one; the outcomes are attitudes toward the other party and toward democratic norms. Party leaners are kept but true independents and 'other party' identifiers were screened out at recruitment, so the sample is effectively two-party. The sample is old (mean age ~57) relative to a census quota panel. Each respondent was randomly assigned to one of 25 short interventions, an active/placebo control, or a no-treatment control that is oversampled ~5x. All outcomes are 0-100 sliders measured once, after treatment. Nearly every outcome item is piped with the respondent's own in-party and out-party, so the same column means 'Republicans' for a Republican and 'Democrats' for a Democrat.

- PA | 0-100 | Partisan Animosity - composite of feeling thermometers toward out-party voters/politicians and a 0-50c dictator game, reverse-scored so HIGHER = MORE animosity
- ADA | 0-100 | Support for Undemocratic Practices - mean of 4 0-100 agree items (reduce polling stations in out-party areas; governors ignore court rulings; prosecute journalists; do not accept election results)
- SPV | 0-100 | Support for Partisan Violence - mean of 4 0-100 items on how OK/justified threats, online harassment and political violence by in-partisans are
- SUC | 0-100 | Support for Undemocratic Candidates - mean of 4 0-100 vote-choice items: would you still vote for your own party's candidate if that candidate endorsed each undemocratic practice
- OppBip | 0-100 | Opposition to Bipartisan Cooperation - 100 minus the mean of two 0-100 items on wanting the parties to work together and to compromise (reverse-scoring ALREADY applied in the file; verified exactly)
- SocDistrust | 0-100 | Social Distrust - 100 minus the single 0-100 generalised-trust item ('most people can be trusted' vs 'you need to be very careful'); reverse-scoring already applied (verified exactly)
- SocDis | 0-100 | Social Distance - 100 minus the mean of two 0-100 items on comfort with out-party close friends and neighbours; reverse-scoring already applied (verified exactly)
- BEPF | 0-100 | Biased Evaluation of Politicized Facts - composite of 8 0-100 items (4 Republican-favourable, 4 Democrat-favourable factual claims); higher = more partisan-biased evaluation
- Composite | 0-100 | Composite of Outcomes - the study's own pre-computed overall index of antidemocratic attitudes and partisan animosity, higher = worse

XOVS_T1_1C In 1898, after the Spanish-American War, Puerto Rico became a U.S. territory as
a part of the treaty resolving the conflict. In 1917, Congress passed the Jones Act which granted
all Puerto Ricans born after April 25th, 1898, U.S. citizenship. Since the end of the Spanish-
American war, Puerto Rico has operated under “territorial status” in relation to the United
States. Puerto Rico does not have any electoral votes, nor any voting representation in
Congress, although they have a single non-voting representative. During its time as a territory of
the United States, multiple popular movements for independence and/or statehood have arisen
and gained varied support from the Puerto Rican population overall. Starting in the second half
of the 20th century, Puerto Rico started holding periodic referendums on the political status of
the island. While most Puerto Ricans have historically supported a change in status, only in the
most recent 2012 referendum did a majority support a change in status (54%), and only 61% of
those in favor supported statehood. Some believe that Puerto Ricans, as U.S. citizens, deserve
statehood for their home and view the island’s continued territorial status as hypocritical
considering the anti-colonial past of the United States. Others point to the economic challenges
of admitting a debt-stricken state with high poverty rates and the fact that a new state would
upset the electorate as reasons against statehood.

Nonviolent protests are twice as likely to succeed as armed conflicts – and those
engaging a threshold of 3.5% of the population have never failed to bring about change.
There are, of course, many ethical reasons to use nonviolent strategies. But compelling
research by Erica Chenoweth, a political scientist at Harvard University, conﬁrms that civil
disobedience is not only the moral choice; it is also the most powerful way of shaping world
politics – by a long way.     Chenoweth performed an extensive review of the literature on civil
resistance and social movements from 1900 to 2006 – a data set then corroborated with other
experts in the ﬁeld. By the end of this process, they had collected data from 323 violent and
nonviolent campaigns. And their results were striking.     Looking at hundreds of campaigns over
the last century, Chenoweth found that nonviolent campaigns are twice as likely to achieve their
goals as violent campaigns.      Strength in numbers    This was partly the result of strength in
numbers. Chenoweth argues that nonviolent campaigns are more likely to succeed because
they can recruit many more participants from a much broader demographic. Overall, the
nonviolent campaigns attracted around four times as many participants (200,000) as the
average violent campaign (50,000).
“Numbers really matter for building power in ways that can really pose a serious challenge or
threat to entrenched authorities or occupations,” Chenoweth says – and nonviolent protest
seems to be the best way to get that widespread support. Chenoweth cites many reasons that
nonviolent protests can garner such high levels of support. Perhaps most obviously, violent
protests necessarily exclude people who abhor and fear bloodshed, whereas peaceful
protesters maintain the moral high ground.    Chenoweth points out that nonviolent protests also
have fewer physical barriers to participation. You do not need to be ﬁt and healthy to engage in
a strike, whereas violent campaigns tend to lean on the support of physically ﬁt young men. And
while many forms of nonviolent protests also carry serious risks – just think of China’s response
in Tiananmen Square in 1989 – Chenoweth argues that nonviolent campaigns are generally
easier to discuss openly, which means that news of their occurrence can reach a wider
audience. Violent movements, on the other hand, require a supply of weapons, and tend to rely
on more secretive underground operations that might struggle to reach the general population.
By engaging broad support across the population, nonviolent campaigns are also more likely
to win support among the police and the military – the very groups that the government should
be leaning on to bring about order. During a peaceful street protest of millions of people, the
members of the security forces may also be more likely to fear that their family members or
friends are in the crowd – meaning that they fail to crack down on the movement.
“There are more options for engaging in nonviolent resistance that don’t place people in as
much physical danger, particularly as the numbers grow, compared to armed activity,”
Chenoweth says. “And the techniques of nonviolent resistance are often more visible, so that it's
easier for people to ﬁnd out how to participate directly, and how to coordinate their activities for
maximum disruption.”     A magic number?
In Chenoweth’s data set, it was only once the nonviolent protests had achieved that 3.5%
threshold of active engagement that success seemed to be guaranteed – and raising even that
level of support is no mean feat. In the US, it would involve 11 million citizens – more than the
total population of New York City.

| Once around 3.5% of the whole population has begun to participate actively, success appears
to be inevitable.    The fact remains, however, that nonviolent campaigns are the only reliable
