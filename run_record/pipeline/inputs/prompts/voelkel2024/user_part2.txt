Q3EV_Inst_4 The six "taste buds" of morality
There's a useful metaphor to understand what's causing these differences in worldviews: the
mind is like a tongue with six taste buds.
We all have the same taste buds on our tongues (salty, sweet, sour, bitter, and savory), but
we all have different taste preferences. Some of us have a strong sweet tooth; others prefer
more salty foods.
Each culture ends up generating its own cuisine with its own flavors and seasonings. And
even within any given culture, people vary.     You will be able to proceed to the next page after
the estimated reading time has elapsed.

Q3EV_Inst_5 Moral Foundations Theory  A team of psychologists applied this metaphor to
morality and developed a theory called Moral Foundations Theory to explain how we develop
our moral worldviews.
The idea is that we all share a set of innate and universal moral foundations, which serve as
the building blocks of morality — just as our taste buds serve as the building blocks of our food
preferences.

You will be able to proceed to the next page after the estimated reading time has elapsed.

Q3EV_Inst_6 Similarly to how we form our taste preferences, we form our moral
worldview through a combination of many factors:     We all begin with the same moral
foundations, which stem from our shared evolutionary heritage   Our worldview is then
influenced by the cultures in which we are raised   Our specific upbringing influences how our
worldview develops   Our own unique life experiences continue to inform our worldview
We all end up with worldviews that are as unique as our fingerprints!
While we can agree on a lot of things with other people, no two people will agree on absolutely
everything.

You will be able to proceed to the next page after the estimated reading time has elapsed.

Q3EV_Inst_7 How does this play out in real life?
Our worldview becomes a lens through which we interpret the world around us. When we
encounter information that has to do with morality, our brain has an instant reaction based on
our moral worldview (that's our brain moving at lightning speed to make sense of the situation).
The problem is, once our brain imposes an interpretation of the situation, we can become
absolutely convinced that that's the only way to see it. Based on our particular worldview, we
might interpret the situation as "Yanny" but someone with a different worldview might see it as
"Laurel."

That's where Moral Foundations Theory comes in handy. It can help us decode other people's
worldviews to understand what's driving their beliefs and find a common language. But what are
the 6 moral foundations?

You will be able to proceed to the next page after the estimated reading time has elapsed.

Q3EV_Inst_8a 1. Care  The Care foundation serves as the basis for caring for others and trying
to prevent harm. This underlies the values of kindness and compassion.
A person who relies heavily on the Care foundation would probably prioritize being gentle and
kind.

A person who doesn't rely on this foundation very much is more likely to value being tough and
independent.

Q3EV_Inst_8b 2. Fairness

This foundation underlies the values of justice, equality, and reciprocity. It's also connected to

Q3EV_Inst_9a 3. Loyalty    The Loyalty foundation underlies the drive to be loyal to groups that

This foundation also forms the basis of values like patriotism, being a team player, and self-
sacrifice for the sake of the group.

Q3EV_Inst_9b 4. Authority  The Authority foundation underlies the value of

foundation.

You will be able to proceed to the next page after the estimated reading time has elapsed.

Q3EV_Inst_10a 5. Sanctity
The Sanctity foundation underlies the notion that certain things are "pure" or sacred, and that
they should be protected or treated with reverence.

This can manifest in treating objects and beliefs as sacred. It can also underlie the notion of
treating the human body like a temple that must not be desecrated.

Q3EV_Inst_10b 6. Liberty  The Liberty foundation underlies our desire for autonomy — the
freedom to make our own decisions. This serves as the basis for the ideal of individual freedom,
as well as the desire to eliminate oppression.

[46OS - Empathy Beliefs]
46OS_Inst_1 Think about the last time you debated a ${e://Field/Outparty_Person} on an issue

46OS_Inst_2 Discussing issues with ${e://Field/Outparty_Person}s can be hard. Our gut

Actively trying to empathize with ${e://Field/Outparty_Person}s may seem not only tiring, but

46OS_Inst_3 In one study, researchers asked participants to try to convince a person who
disagreed with them on the topic of gun control. Researchers found that those motivated to
empathize with the opposing person's perspective were seen as more persuasive and were
better liked than those who weren't as motivated to empathize.

You will be able to proceed to the next page after the estimated reading time has elapsed.

46OS_Inst_4 Relatedly, large-scale fieldwork in political science has shown that
nonjudgmentally exchanging personal stories with those who we disagree with can be a
very effective way of changing people's minds around contentious issues.

The researchers trained door-to-door canvassers to talk about divisive issues, such as
immigration policy in the U.S., by first listening to what voters had to say and then sharing their
own thoughts and personal experiences. This method encouraged open one-to-one
conversations and moved away from the more traditional tactic of just presenting voters with
facts and statistics. It was also more productive: this canvassing approach was estimated to be
102 times more effective than classic political persuasion strategies like TV and radio
advertising, direct mail, or phone banking.

You will be able to proceed to the next page after the estimated reading time has elapsed.

46OS_Inst_5 This empathic exchange of ideas can foster civil debates and inspire openness to
new information - which are important aspects of a healthy democracy. As the research shows,
this doesn't mean giving up on your own beliefs. After all, you don't need to agree with
someone's opinion to understand it.
Given these findings, empathizing with those who disagree with us may seem effortful, but it is
a powerful way to build consensus and find common ground across divides.
