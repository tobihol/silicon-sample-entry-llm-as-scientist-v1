climate change is happening. Some politically-motivated groups use misleading tactics to try to 
convince the public that there is a lot of disagreement among scientists. However, scientific

humans are causing climate change”. One such politically motivated group claims to have

“there is no convincing scientific evidence that human release of carbon dioxide, methane or 
other greenhouse gases is causing or will, in the foreseeable future, cause catastrophic heating

prove that there is no scientific consensus on human-caused climate change. This may sound

Climate or Atmospheric Science. Simply calling yourself a “scientist” does not make someone 
an expert in climate science. By contrast, 97% of actual climate scientists agree that human-
caused climate change is happening.

### Consensus Framing 2
The Scientific Consensus on Global Warming Climate research shows that the temperature 
of the Earth has been increasing every decade since the 1970s. It is also established that the 
burning of fossil fuels releases heat-trapping greenhouse gases into the atmosphere. 
Greenhouse gas emissions have been directly linked to global warming and climate change. A 
recent survey of scientists has found that the greater their expertise in climate science, the 
stronger their agreement on human-caused global warming. Among climate scientists who are 
actively publishing research into climate, at least 98 out of 100 agree that global warming is a 
direct consequence of the burning of fossil fuels by humans. Overwhelming agreement is also 
found in published climate research. An analysis of 21 years of climate research found that 
97.1% of relevant papers agree with the consensus. Papers rejecting the consensus are a 
vanishingly small minority. Surveys of the climate science community and analysis of 
published climate research both find the same result. Based on the evidence, at least 98% of 
climate scientists have concluded that human-caused climate change is happening. Fossil fuel 
and political groups try to cast doubt on climate science, in order to delay regulation of the fossil 
fuel industry. They do this by manufacturing the appearance of an ongoing scientific debate. 
The “fake debate” strategy was pioneered by the tobacco industry in the 1970s. They used 
doctors and scientists to reassure the public that smoking didn’t cause health problems. These 
spokespeople were either non-experts or among the small minority of scientists who dissented 
from the scientific consensus that smoking is bad for you. However, in the public’s eye, this 
conveyed the appearance of ongoing scientific debate. The “Tobacco Strategy” is being used 
again, but this time to cast doubt on climate science. Less than 2% of climate scientists 
disagree with the consensus position that humans are causing global warming. However, when 
the media present the views of a dissenting scientist alongside a mainstream scientist, the 
public comes away with the mistaken impression of a 50:50 debate. Ironically, the journalistic 
standard of giving both sides equal weight has ended up distorting the state of climate science.

### Gains Framing
Overall, by mitigating climate change, we will maintain current ecosystems and 
biodiversity, reduce the likelihood of experiencing extreme weather, reduce losses in ice and 
snow cover, and prevent further sea level rises. Benefits include preventing the loss of animal 
and plant life, reducing human mortality rates from extreme weather events, reducing the 
spread of disease, maintaining food and water supplies, reducing migration and related violent 
conflict, protecting infrastructure, community, cultural sites, and jobs. Ecosystems and 
biodiversity With large reductions in carbon emissions, animal species and 
ecosystems will be maintained, and some saved. By preserving the landscape and 
preventing the loss of animal and plant life, jobs will be saved and food supplies maintained. By 
preventing further temperature changes, plants and animals will maintain their seasonal cycles, 
e.g., nesting, and remain in current geographical areas. This is likely to save many trees 
species, reduce coral reef bleaching, save the lives of animals dependent on sea ice, and avoid 
mass deaths from heatwaves. Preventing further changes caused by climate change in 
the nature and extent of current ecosystems will maintain the amount of carbon that 
ecosystems can store. By reducing climate changes, we will reduce wildfires, death of trees, 
increases in insect populations, the drying up of peatland, and thaws in permafrost. Similarly in 
coastal areas, preventing sea levels from rising further and the number and extent of heatwaves 
will preserve mangroves, marshes, and seagrass areas. These areas currently naturally absorb 
carbon and the storing of this carbon will mitigate further climate changes. Food systems and 
security Mitigating climate change will reduce pressure on food production. By reducing 
climate impacts, e.g., floods, droughts, we can protect food production. This will maintain the 
availability of food and reduce disparities in availability of food between regions, preventing an 
increase in poverty in many areas. Reducing climate change will also reduce malnutrition by 
maintaining access to quality and a variety of food. Sea levels and water systems By

mitigating climate change we can prevent predicted changes in the water cycle, water 
quality, ice coverage, droughts and flood. Reducing climate change could help between 800 
million to 4 billion people (depending on the extent of warming) to maintain water supplies. 
Action on climate change will mean the avoidance of glacier mass loss, permafrost thaw and 
decline in snow cover. This will also help to avoid increases in floods, and landslides, and 
maintain water availability to prevent severe consequences for people, infrastructure, and the 
economy in many regions. Mitigating climate change will reduce sea level rise and will 
protect ecosystems, people, livelihoods, infrastructure, food security, cultural and

distress associated with worry about climate change. Communities and migration By 
reducing the number of likely extreme weather events, e.g., flooding, many people in

have to move because of changes to livelihoods impacted by climate changes. This has positive 
implications for maintaining social cohesion, communities, and culture. It will also prevent the 
amount of people living in extreme poverty from increasing. Reducing climate change will 
restrict the resulting likelihood of humanitarian crises which will avoid increases in violent 
conflict, due to related forced migration and scarcity of resources.

head without turning his whole body. There were even reports of cravats worn so thick that they 
stopped sword thrusts. The various styles knew no bounds, as cravats of tasseled strings, plaid 
scarves, tufts and bows of ribbon, lace and embroidered linen all had their staunch adherents. 
How can we account for the continued popularity of neckties? For years, fashion historians and 
sociologists predicted their demise - the one element of a man's attire with no obvious function. 
Perhaps they are merely part of an inherited tradition. As long as world leaders continue to wear 
ties, the youth of the world will follow suit and ties will remain a key component to any man's 
professional wardrobe. Moreover, the adaptability of neckties to various styles and occasions 
ensures their enduring relevance in the ever-evolving landscape of men's fashion.
